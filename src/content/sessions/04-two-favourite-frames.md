## Before the session

Bring a phone or camera and wear clothing that makes the outline of the torso
and legs easy to compare. Enable grid lines. If the device has several optical
focal lengths, know how to switch between them without using digital zoom by
accident.

## In the session

### 1. Distance test

Make a close portrait from too near, then step back and recover a similar crop
with a longer optical view or by leaving more space around the person. Compare
nose, ears, jaw and the overall feeling of recognition.

### 2. Height test

Keep the subject and background fixed. Make full-body portraits from a high,
eye-level and waist-level camera position. Keep one version level and make one
deliberately tilted version. Compare head size, leg length and stance.

### 3. Framing test

Produce one full-body, one half-body and one close-up portrait. Scan all four
edges and avoid cutting unintentionally through ankles, knees, elbows or wrists.

### 4. Subject selection

The subject chooses the frame that looks most like them before the photographer
shares a preference. Discuss the visible reason for agreement or disagreement.

## Afterwards

Submit a nine-frame contact sheet labelled with distance, camera height and
framing. Add three short conclusions:

1. the position that produced the most natural face;
2. the position that produced the preferred full-body proportion; and
3. one deliberately distorted version that could still work for a specific
   style.

The strongest result is not automatically the longest legs or the blurriest
background. It is the frame whose proportion supports how the subject wants to
be seen.
