## Editing Should Complete the Photograph You Made

The shoot already established light, perspective, composition, movement and
style. Post-production should strengthen those decisions and make the sequence
coherent. It should not be the first moment the photograph receives an idea.

> Edit the photograph until the intended feeling becomes clear—not until the
> person becomes somebody else.

Week 12 completes the whole process: adjust the selected files, agree on
retouching boundaries, make the series consistent, prepare the delivery, ask
the subject for feedback and compare the result with the beginning of the
course.

## Begin With a Clean Technical Pass

Work from the selected sequence rather than editing the entire shoot.

### Exposure

Adjust overall brightness while protecting important facial highlights and
keeping intentional shadow. Do not brighten every night image until it looks
like daytime or darken every cinematic image until the face disappears.

### White balance

Correct an unwanted colour cast, especially on skin. Then decide whether the
image should remain neutral, warmer or cooler for the intended feeling. Skin
does not need to be grey-neutral when the whole environment is clearly lit by
sunset, a warm interior or coloured night light.

### Contrast

Use contrast to clarify shape and mood. Excessive contrast can deepen eye
shadows and make skin texture harsh; too little can remove the separation
created during the shoot.

### Colour

Control dominant colours rather than increasing every saturation slider. Check
that clothing, background and skin still belong to the same scene. A style is
usually easier to see when a few colours lead and the rest support them.

### Crop and straightening

Level horizons and remove edge distractions. Keep the framing decisions from
Week 4 and Week 6: avoid accidental joint crops, allow useful looking space and
preserve environmental context when it is part of the memory.

## Protect Skin Tone Before Chasing a Look

Skin should remain believable inside the chosen style. Compare the face across
the series rather than editing each image in isolation.

Watch for:

- skin becoming too orange, magenta, green or grey;
- one image being much brighter than the rest;
- selective colour changes affecting lips, hair or clothing unexpectedly;
- shadows around the eyes becoming heavier after contrast; and
- smoothing removing texture until the face looks artificial.

Use a reference image from the same lighting condition and return to it while
editing. Consistency does not require every frame to have identical colour;
daylight, interior and night scenes can differ while still belonging together.

## Retouch Temporary Distractions Carefully

Some edits remove short-lived or accidental details without changing identity.
With the subject's agreement, these may include:

- a temporary blemish;
- a stray hair crossing the face;
- lint, a small clothing mark or makeup fallout;
- sensor dust;
- a minor background distraction; or
- a bright edge object the photographer failed to remove on location.

Keep the original and use non-destructive tools where possible. Zoom out
frequently. An edit that looks precise at 300% may look obvious or unnatural at
normal viewing size.

## Changing the Body Is a Different Decision

Slimming the face, lengthening legs, changing eye size, narrowing the waist,
reshaping the nose or altering body proportions does more than clean a file. It
changes the person.

Do not assume that “flattering” gives permission to reshape. Ask what the
subject wants, explain what the edit would change and be prepared not to do it.
The request should come from an informed conversation, not from the
photographer deciding that a real feature is a defect.

Use earlier course skills first:

- perspective and distance from Week 4;
- light from Week 5;
- background and framing from Week 6;
- exposure and movement from Week 7; and
- selection from Week 11.

Those decisions create a flattering portrait without requiring the software to
invent different anatomy.

:::tip[The recognition test]
If the subject notices the edit before the photograph, or no longer feels that
the face or body is theirs, the edit has crossed an important boundary.
:::

## AI Editing Follows the Same Boundary

Course policy permits AI-assisted editing of a photograph the student captured.
It may help adjust exposure, colour, noise, sharpness or local detail. Keep the
original file and acknowledge substantial AI assistance in the required note.

Do not submit an image generated from scratch instead of a photograph you made.
Do not use AI to fabricate or change camera settings, capture parameters or
EXIF metadata. A tool becoming automatic does not remove the photographer's
responsibility for the result or the subject's right to approve how she is
represented.

## Edit the Series, Not Twelve Separate Photographs

A final set should feel related without forcing every file through one preset.

1. Edit one anchor photograph that best represents the desired result.
2. Match neighbouring photographs for broad exposure, white balance and colour
   relationship.
3. Preserve justified differences between locations or times of day.
4. Review the sequence at thumbnail size for rhythm and consistency.
5. Review faces at useful size for skin, focus and retouching.
6. Remove any image that only repeats a stronger one.

The anchor creates direction. Individual adjustments protect the truth of each
lighting condition.

## Prepare the Final Delivery

Before exporting, confirm:

- the final sequence and order;
- which images may remain private, be submitted, shared online or used in a
  portfolio;
- suitable resolution, colour space and file format for the destination;
- consistent file names and orientation;
- whether both colour and black-and-white versions are genuinely needed; and
- where the original files and final exports will be backed up.

Permission to submit the series is not automatically permission to publish it
elsewhere. Keep those decisions separate.

## Ask for Feedback After the Photographs Are Finished

Assessment 03 requires an interview with the subject after the final images are
complete. Do not ask only, “Do you like them?” Use questions that produce
evidence:

- Which photograph feels most like you, and why?
- Which photograph would you choose to share?
- Is there any image you do not want included?
- Did the final style match what we agreed before the shoot?
- Did any edit change your appearance in a way you dislike?
- When did the process feel most and least comfortable?
- What should I repeat or change next time?

Record the feedback accurately. Do not turn a mixed response into universal
satisfaction or pressure the subject to protect the photographer's feelings.

## Compare Week 1 With Week 12

Return to an early photograph and place it beside the final series. Analyse the
change across the whole process.

| Week 1 question | Week 12 evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Did you know what she liked? | The brief, references and joint selection show shared criteria |
| Did she look like herself? | Distance, angle, light and restrained editing protect recognition |
| Did the location help? | Background, composition and depth support the person |
| Could you control the result? | Aperture, shutter, exposure and style choices are intentional |
| Did she feel comfortable? | Direction, movement, breaks and feedback document the experience |
| Does the set remember the day? | Environment, portrait, action and detail create a complete sequence |

Self-reflection should identify both improvement and remaining limits. The
highest-level work is not a claim that every problem has disappeared. It is
evidence that the photographer can see problems, make choices and respond to
the person rather than defend the camera.

## The Complete Course Process

1. Learn what she likes.
2. Plan a day she can enjoy.
3. Carry equipment that suits the day.
4. Make her proportions feel natural.
5. Put her in useful light.
6. Build a clean, meaningful frame.
7. Control depth, movement and exposure.
8. Design a style from visible decisions.
9. Communicate the idea clearly.
10. Help her move and feel comfortable.
11. Select the story together.
12. Edit and deliver without replacing her.

> A technically strong image is not enough if the person in it does not
> recognise herself, approve how she is represented or want to keep the
> photograph.
