## A Good Subject Cannot Rescue a Careless Frame

The light is soft and the expression is good, but a pole appears to grow from
the subject's head. A rubbish bin sits beside the dress. A bright stranger at
the edge of the frame attracts more attention than the face.

These are not editing problems. They are signs that the photographer saw the
person but did not see the whole picture.

> Before searching for an advanced composition, make the frame straight,
> clean and intentional.

Week 6 builds a reliable visual order: place the person clearly, remove or
control distractions, then use foreground, depth and perspective to make the
ordinary location feel deliberate.

## Begin With Four Simple Structures

### Centre composition

Place the person near the centre when symmetry, direct eye contact or a strong
background shape supports it. Centre composition is not automatically boring.
It becomes weak when the person is centred without checking everything around
them.

Use it for doorways, corridors, roads, architectural symmetry and portraits
where the face should feel immediate.

### Rule of thirds

Place the face or body near one of the third lines and allow the environment to
occupy the remaining space. This works when the location contributes meaning
or when the person is looking or moving into the open part of the frame.

The grid is a starting aid, not a law. Do not push the person onto a third if it
creates a worse background or an awkward empty area.

### Symmetry

Symmetry makes a frame feel calm, formal and controlled. Stand on the real
centre line, make the camera level and check that both sides of the background
carry similar visual weight. Almost-symmetry can look more accidental than a
clearly asymmetric composition.

### Negative space

Empty wall, sky, water or shadow can give the person room and create a quiet or
minimal feeling. The empty area should support the subject's direction, scale
or mood. Space that contains random signs, branches and people is not visually
empty simply because the subject is small.

## Straight and Clean Comes First

Use grid lines to check horizons, walls and vertical structures. A tilted frame
can create energy when deliberate; a nearly level horizon usually looks like a
mistake.

Before pressing the shutter, scan in this order:

1. the face and expression;
2. the outline of the hair and body;
3. the background immediately behind the head and shoulders;
4. all four frame edges; and
5. the brightest and most colourful objects in the scene.

This short scan catches most avoidable distractions without slowing the shoot
into a technical inspection.

## Read the Background Before Placing the Person

Do not place the subject first and then accept whatever happens behind them.
Search for a background that already has useful structure.

Common distractions include:

- strangers crossing behind the body;
- cars, bins, signs and temporary barriers;
- poles, trees or building edges touching the head;
- bright patches that compete with the face;
- high-contrast lines cutting through the neck or limbs;
- clutter at the edge of the frame; and
- colours that fight the clothing or skin tone.

The solution is often one step sideways, a slightly higher or lower camera, a
tighter frame, more distance from the background or ten seconds of waiting.
Editing should not be the first plan for a problem the photographer could see.

:::tip[Move yourself first]
When the background is messy, ask what changes if the photographer moves left,
right, higher, lower, closer or farther. Do not make the subject repeatedly
relocate while the camera stays in the least useful position.
:::

## Separate the Person From the Background

Clear separation does not require maximum blur. It can come from several
relationships:

### Tone

A bright face against a darker wall, or a dark silhouette against a bright sky,
creates separation through luminance.

### Colour

Clothing that contrasts with the environment can clarify the figure. Similar
colours can instead create a quiet, unified style if the face remains clear.

### Light

A rim of backlight, a brighter patch on the face or a darker background can
separate the outline.

### Distance

Move the subject away from the background. This reduces visible clutter,
changes its scale and makes optical blur easier when the lens and aperture
allow it.

### Focus and depth of field

A wider aperture can soften detail, but it cannot repair poor placement. A
large bright bin blurred behind the shoulder may still be a large bright bin.

Use the simplest combination that makes the person immediately readable.

## Do Not Make Every Location a Tourist Photo

The default tourist frame places the landmark in the centre, places the person
directly in front of it, then steps back until both fit. The result proves that
the person visited the location but may say little about how it felt.

Try changing one relationship:

- move sideways until the landmark becomes a clean shape rather than a head
  decoration;
- let the person interact with the place instead of standing still in front of
  it;
- use a closer portrait with one recognisable detail of the location;
- place the person at a different scale within the scene;
- photograph along a street, path or wall to create depth; or
- wait for a moment that belongs to the activity.

The location can remain recognisable without occupying every part of the
frame.

## Foreground Creates Depth

An object close to the camera can frame the subject and create a sense of
looking into a scene. Useful foregrounds include leaves, a doorway, a window
edge, a table, fabric, reflections or a nearby wall.

Foreground must support the person rather than hide them accidentally. Check
that it does not cross the eyes or create an unexplained dark shape. Use it
lightly at first: one foreground layer, one subject layer and one background
layer are enough to make space feel three-dimensional.

## Perspective Organises Space

Roads, paths, railings, walls and rows of lights can guide attention toward the
person. This is often called using leading lines, but the line only works if it
actually leads somewhere useful.

Camera position controls these relationships. A small sideways movement can
make lines converge behind the subject; a lower position can bring pavement or
flowers into the foreground; a higher position can simplify a cluttered
background.

Combine these decisions with Week 4. A dramatic camera position may improve the
space while damaging facial or body proportions. The composition is successful
only when both the person and the scene benefit.

## Depth of Field Is One Layer of the Decision

Shallow depth of field can simplify a background and create softness. Greater
depth can show the place and make the portrait feel documentary or energetic.

Ask what the background is supposed to contribute:

- If the place is irrelevant, simplify it through position, distance and
  aperture.
- If the place explains the memory, keep enough detail to recognise it.
- If the background is attractive but busy, separate the person before using
  moderate blur.

Do not turn every environment into colour circles. Atmosphere sometimes needs
information.

## Build the Frame in a Reliable Order

1. Decide whether the picture is about the person alone or the person in a
   place.
2. Choose a simple structure: centre, thirds, symmetry or negative space.
3. Check the camera is level where it should be.
4. Scan the background and frame edges.
5. Move the photographer to remove distractions.
6. Separate the person through tone, colour, light, distance or focus.
7. Add foreground or stronger perspective only when it improves the story.
8. Check the person's face and proportions again.

> A strong composition does not make the location look complicated. It makes
> the photographer's decision easy to see.

Give her a frame that is clean enough to notice her first and specific enough
to remember where the photograph happened.
