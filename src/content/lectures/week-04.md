## Why Can Someone Look Better in Person Than in the Photograph?

The person in front of you has not suddenly changed. The camera has translated
a three-dimensional face and body into a flat frame from one exact position.
If that position is too close, too low, too high or poorly aligned, the result
can exaggerate features and proportions that never felt noticeable in person.

This is why “not photogenic” is often the wrong diagnosis. The photographer may
simply be standing in the wrong place.

> Before asking your partner to change her face or body, change the camera's
> position.

Week 4 focuses on making a person look natural, recognisable and good before
trying to make the portrait unusual. Style can come later. First, the picture
should still feel like the person who agreed to be photographed.

## Distance Changes Perspective

When the camera moves close to a face, the nearest features become much larger
relative to features farther away. The nose may appear larger, the ears may
seem smaller, and the sides of the face may fall away. Moving farther back
reduces those differences and usually produces more familiar facial
proportions.

Focal length affects how much of the scene fits in the frame. Perspective is
primarily controlled by camera position and distance. A wide lens becomes
unflattering when it encourages the photographer to move very close; a longer
lens often looks calmer because the photographer has stepped farther away to
make the same crop.

:::tip[Fix the distance before the face]
If a close portrait makes the face look unfamiliar, step back first. Then use a
longer focal length or crop to recover the framing.
:::

The same principle applies to full-body portraits. A camera very close to the
upper body can make the head and torso dominate while the legs recede. A camera
close to the feet can enlarge shoes and legs. More working distance makes the
proportions less extreme and gives the photographer more control.

## Camera Height Changes the Body

There is no single correct height, but every height has a visible consequence.

### Around eye level

Eye level is a reliable starting point for head-and-shoulders portraits. It
keeps the face familiar and makes communication easy. Raising the camera far
above the eyes can make the forehead and top of the head dominate while the jaw
and body become smaller.

### Around chest or waist level

For half-body and full-body portraits, lowering the camera toward the chest or
waist often balances the torso and legs. It can lengthen the lower body without
making the result feel obviously distorted. Keep the camera level unless the
tilt is deliberate.

### Very low

A low viewpoint can make the subject feel taller and more powerful. Used
carelessly, it can also enlarge the feet, shorten the upper body and show an
unwanted view under the chin. “Low angle makes legs longer” is not a complete
rule; distance, lens choice, pose and camera tilt still matter.

### Very high

A high viewpoint can feel intimate or playful and may simplify the background.
For a standing full-body portrait it usually shortens the legs and enlarges the
head. Use that result because it suits the image, not by accident.

## Vertical Tilt Changes Proportion Too

When the camera is tilted sharply upward or downward, lines that were parallel
in the scene begin to converge in the photograph. The part of the body nearest
the lens grows while the far end shrinks.

For a natural full-body result:

- step far enough back to include the person without extreme lens distortion;
- begin around waist height;
- keep the camera close to level; and
- use a longer view or crop instead of tilting aggressively to fit the frame.

Once that neutral version works, you can deliberately lower, raise or tilt the
camera to change the subject's energy.

## Horizontal Angle Changes the Face

A face is not perfectly symmetrical, and people often have a side or angle
they prefer. This is not a universal beauty rule. It is information about one
person.

Test three simple positions:

1. face directly toward the camera;
2. body and face turned slightly to one side; and
3. body turned away with the face returned gently toward the lens.

A slight turn can show the line of the jaw and create depth. Too much turn may
hide one eye or make the nose cross the far cheek line. The useful angle depends
on the face, expression, hairstyle, light and what the subject recognises as
herself.

Ask which version she prefers. Do not turn one successful test into a permanent
rule that every future portrait must repeat.

## Full Body, Half Body and Close-Up Need Different Decisions

### Full-body portrait

The entire silhouette, stance and relationship to the location matter. Leave
enough space around the feet and check the camera height carefully. Avoid a
wide lens close to the person unless exaggerated energy is the intention.

### Half-body portrait

The face becomes more important while hands, posture and clothing still carry
meaning. Crop between major joints rather than directly through elbows, wrists
or knees. Watch where the lower frame meets the body; an accidental crop can
make an otherwise careful portrait feel unfinished.

### Close-up

Expression and facial proportion dominate. Step back enough to avoid exaggerated
perspective and pay close attention to focus. A crop through hair or the top of
the head can be deliberate, but a frame that almost includes the whole head
often looks like a mistake.

| Framing | Primary question | Common failure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Full body | Do the proportions and stance feel natural? | Head large, legs short, feet cramped by the edge |
| Half body | Does the crop support the gesture? | Cutting directly through a joint or losing the hands accidentally |
| Close-up | Does the face still feel familiar? | Camera too close, wide-angle distortion, focus on the wrong feature |

## The Edges of the Frame Matter

New photographers concentrate on the eyes and forget what the frame is doing to
the rest of the person. Before pressing the shutter, scan all four edges.

- Is a hand or foot almost included but accidentally cut off?
- Does the frame cut through a joint?
- Is the subject pushed against one edge without a reason?
- Does a wide-angle lens stretch the body near the edge of the image?
- Is there enough space in the direction the person is looking or moving?

Make the crop clearly wider or clearly tighter. The most awkward result is
often the indecisive one.

## Start Neutral, Then Add Style

When the location allows it, make a neutral reference frame first:

1. comfortable working distance;
2. camera near the relevant part of the body;
3. level horizon and camera;
4. simple body angle; and
5. clean full-body, half-body or close-up crop.

Show the frame to your partner. If she feels that it looks like her, you now
have a trustworthy starting point. A lower angle, closer distance or unusual
crop can then become an intentional style choice rather than an unrecognised
mistake.

> The first technical responsibility of a portrait photographer is not to
> transform the person. It is to notice how the camera has transformed them.

The goal this week is simple: make her look like herself—natural, believable
and good—before asking the photograph to do anything more dramatic.
