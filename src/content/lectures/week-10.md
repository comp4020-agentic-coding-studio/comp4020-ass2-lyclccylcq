## Natural Is a State, Not a Pose

Someone can move, talk and smile comfortably until a camera points at them.
Then the shoulders rise, the hands lose their purpose, the face waits for a
command and the smile becomes something held rather than felt.

Telling that person to “act natural” makes the problem worse. She is now trying
to perform an undefined version of herself while being watched.

> The goal is not to turn her into a model. It is to create enough clarity,
> movement and trust for her own comfortable state to return.

Week 10 replaces pose catalogues with small actions. The photographer gives the
body something understandable to do, watches the transitions and encourages
what is working.

## Start With an Action That Has a Reason

Static posing makes every hand, shoulder and expression feel important at the
same time. Action moves attention away from self-monitoring.

Useful starting actions include:

- walk toward a clear destination;
- turn back after passing the photographer;
- adjust hair, a sleeve, collar or bag strap;
- open, hold or close a book;
- sit down and shift toward one side;
- lean against a wall, then push away from it;
- look through a window or toward a sound;
- place a hand in a pocket, then take it out while moving; and
- step onto a mark, pause and look back.

The action should belong to the place or the person. Repeating an empty gesture
twenty times will soon look as artificial as a fixed pose.

## Direct the Transition, Not Only the End Position

Many natural-looking frames happen between instructions: the step before the
subject stops, the moment the hair falls back into place, or the expression
after she thinks the sequence has finished.

Give a beginning, path and end:

1. “Begin beside the wall.”
2. “Walk toward the window at your normal pace.”
3. “On the second step, adjust your sleeve.”
4. “When I say now, look back once and keep walking.”

Use continuous focus and a short burst when the movement reaches its useful
phase. Do not demand that the best transition be frozen and held after it has
already passed.

## Give the Hands a Task

“What do I do with my hands?” is usually a sign that the body has no activity.
Offer one purpose rather than arranging every finger.

- hold a coat, flower, book, drink or bag that belongs to the scene;
- place one hand in a pocket and let the other move;
- adjust jewellery, a cuff or a loose strand of hair;
- touch the edge of a chair before sitting;
- rest the hands together only after the shoulders have relaxed; or
- let the arms swing during a walk.

Check that the task does not hide the face, twist the wrist uncomfortably or
become a nervous movement repeated in every frame.

## Help the Body Lose Its Stiff Symmetry

When people become self-conscious, they often place equal weight on both feet,
lock both knees, square both shoulders and hold both arms in the same way. The
body becomes stable but rigid.

Use small asymmetry:

- shift weight toward one leg;
- let one knee soften;
- turn the body slightly away while the face returns;
- place one shoulder closer to the camera;
- sit on the front or side of a seat instead of squarely in the centre; or
- begin from movement and pause briefly rather than constructing the body from
  nothing.

Do not correct every joint. Change the source of the stiffness—weight,
attention or action—and allow the rest of the body to respond.

## Give the Eyes Somewhere to Go

“Where should I look?” needs a real answer.

- at the photographer's nearest eye;
- toward a window, sign or person outside the frame;
- at the object in the hands;
- down the path of movement;
- away first, then back on a cue; or
- at the camera only after another action has ended.

A gaze should connect to the intended feeling. Direct eye contact can feel
confident or intimate. Looking away can feel private, calm or cinematic. Random
looking around usually reads as uncertainty.

## Do Not Demand a Smile

A held smile becomes tense quickly. If a cheerful expression is important,
create a moment in which it can appear and disappear naturally.

Continue a conversation. Ask a question whose answer carries a memory. Give an
action that is slightly playful. Let the subject look away, then call her back.
Photograph the transition into and out of the expression rather than asking her
to maintain it while settings are changed.

Some portraits do not need a smile. A calm or serious expression is not a
failure when it suits the person and style.

## Two Photography Bibles

The first list is satire: a collection of cruel excuses photographers use to
blame the subject for their own choices. **Never say these lines to a person you
are photographing.**

### The Toxic Photographer's Bible — Never Say These

1. “That is just what you look like.”
2. “Your legs really are that short.”
3. “Your face is that big, so every angle looks big.”
4. “The light is not bad; you are just not very fair.”
5. “Neither side of your face looks good.”
6. “My camera does not lie.”
7. “You are short, so maybe I should only shoot half-body.”
8. “Your face is not working; pull the mask higher.”
9. “Stop smiling. Your smile never works in photos.”
10. “Accept the real you and learn to live with it.”
11. “My camera never tells a lie.”
12. “If you are not photogenic, that is your problem.”
13. “Next to you, even the background looks good.”
14. “It is fine. Sony files can be fixed later.”
15. “You have to trust Sony's dynamic range.”
16. “A camera only records more clearly; it does not have a beauty filter.”
17. “Just tell me whether it is clear.”
18. “I studied photography, not magic.”
19. “I am a photographer, not a magician.”
20. “The camera has reproduced the real you.”
21. “Your beauty subscription has expired.”
22. “There are many beautiful things in the world, but none of them involve you.”
23. “If anything I said hurt you, tell me so I can repeat it.”
24. “I think it looks good. I did not even capture your cakey makeup.”
25. “Your performance is weak. That is not my problem.”
26. “Sony photos are the hardest to delete.”
27. “The shoot is free; deleting a photo costs fifty dollars.”
28. “Are you going to hit me? My action camera is recording everything.”

The encouragement list is not a line-by-line correction of the toxic list. It
is a separate bank of 28 lively things you might genuinely say during a shoot
to help her feel seen, confident and excited.

### The Encouragement Bible — Keep the Energy Up

1. “That angle looks amazing on you.”
2. “Wow—you look incredible in this light.”
3. “Yes, that turn was perfect.”
4. “You absolutely nailed that movement.”
5. “That expression is so you.”
6. “Hold on, this is gorgeous.”
7. “Your eyes look amazing here.”
8. “That little smile was everything.”
9. “You look stunning—do that again.”
10. “This light loves you.”
11. “That pose looks effortless on you.”
12. “Beautiful—keep exactly that energy.”
13. “The way your hair moved just then looked amazing.”
14. “You make this location look expensive.”
15. “That side is looking so good.”
16. “We already have the shot; now we can just have fun.”
17. “You are making this ridiculously easy.”
18. “That was such a good look back.”
19. “Yes! That is the one.”
20. “You look so confident in this frame.”
21. “That movement feels completely natural.”
22. “The camera loves what you are doing right now.”
23. “This might be my favourite one yet.”
24. “You look beautiful—no need to force anything.”
25. “That tiny change made the whole photo.”
26. “You are absolutely glowing.”
27. “We got it, and you look incredible.”
28. “Take a look—you are going to love this one.”

Use the lines naturally, vary them and mean them. The goal here is not to give
another technical instruction; it is to make the person feel that the shoot is
going well and that she is doing brilliantly.

## Know When to Pause

Natural expression disappears when the person is tired, confused or worried
that every frame is failing. Watch for shorter replies, fixed smiles, repeated
apologies, shoulders rising or movements becoming smaller.

Pause to show a successful frame, change the task, offer water, put the camera
away for a while or finish. The photographer does not earn one more sequence by
making the subject responsible for the tension.

## A Simple Movement Sequence

Use this order with someone who feels awkward:

1. begin with walking toward a clear destination;
2. add one hand task such as adjusting a sleeve;
3. add one gaze cue;
4. repeat the movement only once or twice;
5. move into sitting or leaning while the body is already relaxed;
6. make a direct portrait after trust and rhythm have developed; and
7. finish while the subject still feels successful.

> She does not need to become a professional model. The photographer needs to
> become better at creating conditions where an ordinary person can move,
> breathe and recognise herself.
