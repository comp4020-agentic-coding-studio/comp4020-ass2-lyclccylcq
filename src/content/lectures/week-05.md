## Do Not Begin With the Camera Setting

When a face looks tired, uneven or harsh in a photograph, beginners often try
to repair it with exposure, a wider aperture or editing. The faster solution is
often to move the person or the photographer two metres.

Light decides which parts of a face become bright, which fall into shadow, how
visible skin texture becomes and whether the subject needs to squint. Before
changing a setting, look at the face itself.

> Good portrait light is usually found before it is created.

This week is an observation exercise. The goal is to identify where useful
light already exists in streets, parks, buildings and night environments, then
place the person inside it deliberately.

## Read Light With Four Questions

Before photographing, ask:

1. **Direction:** Is the light coming from in front, behind, beside or above?
2. **Hardness:** Are the transitions between light and shadow sharp or soft?
3. **Contrast:** Can the face and background both retain useful detail?
4. **Colour:** Is the light neutral, warm, cool, green or mixed with another
   source?

Look at the shadows under the eyes, nose and chin. Look for a catchlight in the
eyes. Notice whether the subject is squinting or turning away. These clues are
more useful than naming the weather “good” or “bad”.

## Why Midday Top Light Is Difficult

When the sun is high, light falls almost vertically. The forehead and bridge of
the nose receive strong light while the eye sockets, area under the nose and
chin become dark. The result can create:

- empty-looking eyes;
- a hard nose shadow;
- strong contrast across the face;
- more visible skin texture;
- squinting, heat and physical discomfort; and
- an expression that becomes less relaxed with every attempt.

This is the familiar “death top light”. It is not impossible light, but it is a
poor default for a soft, flattering close portrait.

### Move into open shade

The shadow of a building, covered walkway or large awning can remove the direct
top light while the open sky still illuminates the face. Stand near the edge of
the shade and turn the subject toward the brighter open area.

Avoid patchy shade where small gaps in leaves place bright spots across the
forehead and cheeks. Even shade is easier to use than a pattern that changes
whenever the wind moves.

:::tip[Look at the face, not the location label]
“In shade” does not automatically mean good light. Rotate the subject slowly
and stop where the eyes become clear and the facial shadow pattern feels calm.
:::

## Front Light: Bright, Clear and Direct

Front light comes from roughly behind the photographer and illuminates the face
from the camera's direction. It can produce:

- clear facial detail;
- bright, clean colour;
- a vivid blue sky when the sun and exposure cooperate; and
- an energetic, straightforward style.

Soft front light in the morning, late afternoon, overcast weather or open shade
is forgiving. Hard direct front light can make the subject squint and flatten
the face. Do not force somebody to stare toward a bright sun simply because the
sky looks good behind the photographer.

Front light is useful when the photograph should feel open, colourful and
immediate. Check that the photographer's own shadow does not enter the frame.

## Backlight: Put the Sun Behind Her

Backlight places the main light behind the subject. The face is no longer
receiving direct sun, so the expression can relax. Hair and clothing may catch
a bright rim, and the frame can feel soft and atmospheric.

The easiest version does not point directly into an unobstructed sun. Use a
building edge, tree trunk, roof, sign or other object to block the brightest
part of the source while allowing light to pass around it. This reduces flare
and gives the camera a better chance of holding contrast.

Watch for three problems:

- the face becoming too dark against the background;
- flare washing away contrast or focus; and
- a bright background distracting from the person.

Expose for the face, change position until the bright source is controlled,
and accept that some highlight detail may be less important than a good skin
tone.

## Side-Back Light: Shape With a Softer Edge

Side-back light comes from behind and to one side. It can outline the hair and
shoulder while allowing soft light from the sky, a wall or the environment to
reach the face. This often creates more shape than front light and more facial
detail than direct backlight.

Turn the body and face in small amounts. A change of ten degrees can move a nose
shadow, reveal an eye or separate the hair from a dark background. The label
“side-back light” matters less than learning to watch those changes.

## Use the Environment as a Lighting Tool

Cities and buildings constantly modify light.

- A pale wall can reflect soft light into the face.
- A dark entrance can become a clean background while the subject stands near
  its brighter edge.
- A covered arcade can create directional light from one open side.
- A window can act like a large soft source.
- Light-coloured pavement may lift shadows under the chin.
- A tunnel or doorway can frame a bright exit and create controlled direction.

Move the subject toward and away from these boundaries. The distance from a
window, shade edge or reflecting wall changes the quality and intensity of the
light.

## Find a Face Light at Night

Night photography is not only a search for a bright background. The person
needs a source that reaches the face from a useful direction.

Look for:

- shop windows and illuminated displays;
- restaurant or café windows;
- advertising panels and vending machines;
- building entrances;
- light spilling from a doorway;
- streetlights positioned in front of or beside the person; and
- reflected light from pale walls or wet ground.

Place the subject near the light and let the darker street become the
background. Do not stand directly underneath a streetlight if it recreates the
same eye-socket shadows as midday sun.

Mixed colours can be expressive, but they can also make skin difficult to
correct. Begin with one dominant source. Once the face looks intentional, add a
second colour or background light for atmosphere.

## A Small Continuous Light

A small LED light can help when no environmental source reaches the face. A
continuous light is beginner-friendly because both people can see the result
before the photograph is taken.

Keep it simple:

- place it slightly above eye level and off to one side rather than directly
  under the chin;
- bring it closer and lower its power for a softer result;
- match its colour approximately to the surrounding light when possible;
- keep the brightness comfortable; and
- stop if it distracts other people or changes the outing into a production.

Complex flash technique is deliberately left for later learning. The habit to
build now is not “the scene is dark, so fire a flash”. It is “where is the light
already coming from, and where should the person stand?”

## A Simple Search Pattern

At any location, follow this order:

1. Look at the light on the face.
2. Rotate the person toward and away from the brightest area.
3. Move to open shade if overhead light is harsh.
4. Check front, back and side-back positions.
5. Use buildings, trees or signs to block direct sun.
6. At night, bring the face near one useful artificial source.
7. Only then consider adding a small continuous light.

The photographer does not need to command the weather. They need to notice
where the current environment already makes the person look good.

> Do not ask her face to survive bad light. Put her where the light already
> helps.
