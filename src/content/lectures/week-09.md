## The Picture in Your Head Is Not an Instruction

A photographer sees a quiet portrait in a doorway: soft expression, body
turned away, face returning toward the light. The subject hears only, “Stand
there and be natural.” She does not know exactly where to stand, which way to
turn, where to look or when the photographer expects the moment to happen.

When the result does not match the photographer's mental picture, both people
become frustrated. The problem is not a lack of effort. The idea was never
translated into something another person could perform.

> Direction is the skill of turning a visual intention into language the other
> person can understand and choose to follow.

Week 9 begins before the camera is raised. Both people need a basic agreement
about the feeling, purpose and boundaries of the shoot. During the shoot,
instructions should be brief, specific and respectful rather than a stream of
orders.

## Agree on the Photograph Before Directing the Body

Before leaving or beginning the planned photo window, discuss five points.

### Purpose

Is the result for a private memory, a social post, a profile picture, a creative
experiment or the final assessment? A close, clear face may matter for one use;
atmosphere and environment may matter more for another.

### Feeling

Choose two or three words such as bright and playful, quiet and soft, polished
and romantic, or dynamic and urban. Avoid labels so broad that each person
imagines a different result.

### Reference

Use one or two photographs to discuss light, framing, movement and mood. Ask
which parts she likes and which she does not want copied. A reference is a
shared visual vocabulary, not an order to reproduce another person's body or
expression.

### Time and effort

Agree on how long the focused shooting period will last, how many locations or
looks are realistic and when either person can call a break.

### Boundaries

Confirm any angles, clothing, physical actions, public locations or editing
choices that feel uncomfortable. Consent to the overall shoot does not remove
the need to check a new idea.

## Replace Vague Direction With Observable Actions

“Be natural”, “look good”, “relax” and “smile” describe a desired result but do
not explain how to reach it.

| Vague instruction | Actionable direction |
| --- | --- |
| “Stand over there.” | “Put your left foot beside the edge of the doorway.” |
| “Turn a little.” | “Turn your body toward the window, then bring only your face back to me.” |
| “Look natural.” | “Look at the menu, choose one thing you would order, then look back when I say your name.” |
| “Move more.” | “Walk from the bench to the lamp post at your normal pace.” |
| “Do something with your hands.” | “Hold the book at your side, then use one hand to turn a page.” |
| “Smile.” | “Look at me after I count three; you do not need to hold the expression.” |

An instruction becomes useful when the subject knows **where**, **what** and
**when**.

## Give One Change at a Time

“Move left, drop your shoulder, turn your chin, fix your hair, look at me and
walk slowly” is six instructions competing for attention. The subject will
become physically stiff while trying to remember them.

Build the frame in an order:

1. place the feet or destination;
2. set the body direction;
3. give the hands one simple task;
4. choose the gaze; and
5. add movement or expression.

Once the first three are working, stop repeating them. Let the person direct
attention toward the next small change.

:::tip[The subject needs a successful first instruction]
Begin with something easy to understand and perform. A clear success builds
trust; a complicated first pose makes the rest of the session feel like a test.
:::

## Direction Can Describe a Destination

Movement becomes easier when it has a beginning and an end.

- “Walk from me to that window, then stop beside the chair.”
- “Turn toward the street, wait for me to say now, then look back.”
- “Sit on the edge of the bench, place the book beside you, then lean toward it.”
- “Take three slow steps, fix your sleeve on the second, and keep going.”

The destination prevents the subject from asking, “How far?” The timing cue
allows the photographer to anticipate the useful moment rather than demanding
that it be held.

## Demonstrate Without Treating Her Like an Object

When words are difficult, demonstrate the action with your own body. Show the
direction of a turn, the pace of a walk or where the hands might begin. The
demonstration does not need to look elegant; it needs to make the instruction
visible.

Do not move the subject's head, arms, clothing or hair without asking. Say what
you want changed, demonstrate it, or ask, “May I adjust the edge of your
jacket?” Physical correction should never be assumed because the photographer
is concentrating.

## Use Specific Encouragement

“Good” is pleasant but not very informative. Specific feedback tells the
subject what is already working and reduces the feeling that every frame has
failed.

- “That turn opened the light on your eyes—keep that angle.”
- “The way you held the coat looked relaxed; do the same while walking.”
- “We have the clear portrait. Now we can try the more experimental version.”
- “That expression felt like you. You do not need to hold it.”

Do not praise something and immediately replace it with ten corrections. Let a
successful moment remain successful.

## Show Enough, Not Everything

Showing one or two useful frames can confirm that both people are working
toward the same picture. It can also reveal that the subject dislikes an angle
the photographer planned to repeat.

Avoid handing over every frame after every shutter press. Constant review
breaks rhythm and turns small differences into a negotiation before the idea
has had time to develop. Pause at a natural checkpoint:

1. show a clear representative frame;
2. ask what she notices first;
3. identify one adjustment; and
4. continue with a shared goal.

## Communication Runs in Both Directions

The photographer is not the only person allowed to have an idea. Ask:

- “Does this feel like what we discussed?”
- “Is the action comfortable?”
- “Do you want a clearer portrait before we try the strange one?”
- “Would you rather see this angle or change it now?”
- “Do you want one more sequence or are we finished here?”

Listen to the answer even when the light is disappearing. A shared plan is not
shared if only the photographer may revise it.

## A Short Direction Formula

Use this structure when the idea in your head is becoming too complicated:

1. **Place:** “Stand beside the right edge of the window.”
2. **Body:** “Turn your body toward the light.”
3. **Action:** “Adjust your sleeve while taking two steps.”
4. **Gaze:** “Look at your hand, then back to me.”
5. **Timing:** “Start when I say now; you do not need to hold the end.”
6. **Feedback:** “The second turn worked. Keep that pace.”

Not every frame needs all six parts. Use only the information required for the
next action.

> Good direction does not make her obey the photograph in your head. It gives
> both of you a clear way to build and revise that photograph together.
