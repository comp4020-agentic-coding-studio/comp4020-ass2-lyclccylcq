## Decide the Result Before the Setting

Camera controls become confusing when students begin with numbers. Begin with
the picture instead.

- Should the background be readable or soft?
- Should walking, turning or moving hair be frozen or blurred?
- Should the face feel bright and open or dark and atmospheric?

The setting is an answer to one of these questions. It is not the subject of
the photograph.

> Do not ask, “What is the correct setting?” Ask, “What do I want movement,
> depth and brightness to look like?”

Week 7 introduces enough manual control to make those choices deliberately
without turning portrait photography into a mathematics exercise.

## Aperture Controls More Than Brightness

The aperture is the opening inside a lens. A lower f-number such as f/1.8 or
f/2.8 describes a wider opening; a higher number such as f/5.6 or f/8 describes
a smaller opening.

A wider aperture:

- lets more light reach the sensor;
- can produce shallower depth of field;
- helps separate the person from the background; and
- makes accurate focus more important.

A smaller aperture:

- lets in less light;
- keeps more distance in acceptable focus;
- can make movement or paired subjects easier to hold sharp; and
- may require a slower shutter speed or higher ISO.

Depth of field also depends on sensor size, focal length, camera distance and
the distance between subject and background. Aperture is one control, not a
“background blur” button.

### When to open the aperture

Use a wider aperture when the face needs separation, the background is not
important, or the light is low. Check both eyes when the face is turned; the
widest possible setting may leave one eye distractingly soft.

### When to stop down

Use a smaller aperture when the environment explains the picture, the person is
moving toward or away from the camera, or two people need to remain clear.
Sharp background detail is not automatically a failure when the composition is
clean.

## Shutter Speed Controls Time

Shutter speed decides how long the camera records movement. A faster shutter
freezes a shorter slice of time. A slower shutter allows movement to travel
across the image.

### Freeze ordinary movement

Walking, turning, adjusting hair and changing expression all create more motion
than a static pose. As practical starting points—not universal rules—try:

| Action | Starting shutter speed |
| --- | ---: |
| Mostly still portrait | around 1/125 s or faster |
| Walking or a gentle turn | around 1/250 s or faster |
| Fast turn, jump or hair movement | around 1/500 s or faster |

Review the eyes and face at full size. Stabilisation can reduce camera shake,
but it cannot freeze a subject who is moving.

### Use short bursts with action

Continuous shooting can improve the chance of catching the right phase of a
walk, turn or hair movement. Use a short, planned burst around the peak action.
Do not hold the shutter for dozens of nearly identical frames and make the
subject repeat the movement indefinitely.

Anticipation still matters. Give the action a start and end point, watch the
rhythm, then press just before the moment you want.

## Slow Shutter Can Make a Still Portrait Feel Alive

Blur is not only a mistake. A slower shutter can show movement and create a
dreamlike, restless or cinematic feeling.

One approach keeps the subject relatively still while the photographer moves
the camera slightly during the exposure. A small horizontal, vertical or
rotational movement can stretch lights and background detail around the person.
Another approach keeps the camera stable while the subject walks or turns.

Begin safely:

1. make one sharp reference photograph first;
2. try a shutter speed such as 1/15, 1/8 or slower depending on the light;
3. ask the subject to remain still or perform one simple movement;
4. move the camera in one controlled direction; and
5. review whether the face is recognisable enough for the intended style.

Intentional motion blur should have a direction and visual purpose. Random
shake across the eyes is not automatically expressive.

## ISO Supports the Other Decisions

ISO changes how strongly the camera amplifies the captured signal. A higher ISO
can make a practical aperture and shutter speed possible in lower light, but it
may add noise and reduce colour or highlight quality.

For a moving portrait, a slightly noisy sharp face is usually more useful than
a clean but motion-blurred failure. Choose the aperture for depth and the
shutter for movement, then allow ISO to support them within a quality level the
camera can handle.

Auto ISO can be useful when light changes quickly, especially if the camera
lets you set a minimum shutter speed. Automatic does not mean careless when the
photographer has decided which variable the camera may control.

## Metering Is the Camera's Guess

The meter estimates how bright the frame should be. It can be confused by a
bright sky, dark wall, backlight, white clothing or a scene dominated by
shadow. The camera does not know that the face is the priority or that a darker
image is intentional.

Use the preview, histogram or highlight warning where available, but keep the
decision visually grounded:

- Is important facial detail visible?
- Are bright areas on the skin losing all detail?
- Is the background allowed to be darker or brighter than normal?
- Does the exposure support the intended mood?

## Exposure Compensation: Tell Automatic Mode What You Mean

In aperture priority, shutter priority or other automatic modes, exposure
compensation asks the camera for a brighter or darker result while it continues
to meter the scene.

- Add positive compensation when backlight or a bright environment makes the
  face too dark.
- Add negative compensation when dark surroundings make the camera lift the
  scene too much or when bright skin highlights are being lost.
- Return compensation toward zero when the light changes; an old correction
  can quietly ruin the next location.

Make small changes, review the face and stop when the brightness serves the
picture.

## Underexposure and Overexposure Can Be Choices

A bright portrait can feel clean, airy and cheerful. A darker portrait can feel
quiet, cinematic or private. Neither look is created by making every pixel
equally bright.

### A brighter result

Protect important skin highlights and avoid removing all facial shape. Bright
does not require a white sky or clipped forehead.

### A darker result

Keep enough information in the face for the expression to remain intentional.
Dark does not mean the camera accidentally failed to expose the subject.

Judge exposure in relation to skin tone, clothing, background and mood. Do not
try to force every person toward the same meter value or brightness.

## A Practical Order for Portraits

### To freeze movement

1. Choose the shutter speed for the action.
2. Choose an aperture that keeps the necessary parts of the person clear.
3. Let ISO rise enough to support those choices.
4. Make a short burst around the best part of the movement.
5. Check the face, not only the overall preview.

### For a still portrait with background control

1. Choose the aperture for the required depth.
2. Keep the shutter fast enough for the person's small movements.
3. Adjust ISO or exposure compensation.
4. Review facial highlights and focus.

### For intentional blur

1. Make a sharp safety frame.
2. Choose a slower shutter.
3. Decide whether the subject, camera or both will move.
4. Repeat only enough to understand the effect.
5. Select the frame where blur supports rather than hides the person.

## Phones Still Make These Choices

A phone may hide aperture and shutter controls, but the same visual decisions
remain. Use burst mode for action, tap and adjust exposure for the face, use a
supported manual app when appropriate, or create movement by panning in lower
light. Portrait mode changes simulated depth; it does not replace careful
background choice.

Equipment limits may change the method, not the intention.

> Control is not using manual mode for every frame. Control is knowing which
> visual result you want and preventing the camera from choosing a different
> one by accident.
