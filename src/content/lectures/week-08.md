## Style Begins After the Photograph Works

Weeks 4–7 established a foundation: natural proportions, useful light, a clean
frame and enough camera control to decide depth, movement and exposure. Week 8
asks the next question:

> What should this photograph feel like?

Style is not a preset placed on top of an accidental picture. It is the repeated
relationship between light, camera position, composition, colour, exposure,
movement and expression.

A filter can shift colour. It cannot change where the photographer stood, how
the person related to the background, whether the moment felt energetic, or
which light shaped the face.

## Name the Feeling Before the Technique

“Cinematic” or “aesthetic” is too broad to guide a shoot by itself. Begin with
two or three specific qualities:

- bright, playful and spontaneous;
- quiet, soft and close;
- cinematic, tense and atmospheric;
- minimal, clean and controlled; or
- dynamic, imperfect and energetic.

These words create questions the photographer can answer. Does the light need
to be soft or hard? Should the location be visible? Is the frame stable or
tilted? Does the subject look at the camera, move through the scene or appear
absorbed in something else?

## Different Feelings Come From Connected Decisions

| Style direction | Light | Composition and perspective | Exposure and colour | Moment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bright and playful | clear front or soft open light | closer, simple, energetic framing | bright exposure, clean colour | movement, laughter, quick interaction |
| Quiet and soft | window light, open shade or gentle backlight | stable frame, space around the person | restrained contrast, muted colour | stillness, small expression, private attention |
| Cinematic | directional light, practical night sources or controlled shadow | layered depth, stronger viewpoint, environmental context | deliberate shadow, limited colour palette | anticipation, tension or an unfinished action |
| Minimal | even or carefully shaped light | clean geometry, symmetry or negative space | few colours, controlled brightness | simple posture and direct visual emphasis |
| Dynamic | hard light, mixed city light or visible movement | close or angled framing, diagonals, foreground | contrast or intentional blur | walking, turning, hair or clothing movement |

These are not formulas. They show that style appears when several choices point
in the same direction.

## Find → Analyse → Recreate

The fastest way to build taste is not to save more photographs. It is to study
why a saved photograph works, then test those observations in a new situation.

### 1. Find

Choose one reference portrait that both photographer and subject genuinely
like. Select a photograph whose conditions you can reasonably approach. A film
still with a full lighting crew, impossible location and professional model may
be inspiring but teaches little if every visible decision is unavailable.

Use a small set of references with a shared direction rather than a board of
unrelated “nice photos”. Three compatible images are more useful than thirty
contradictory ones.

### 2. Analyse

Do not begin with the colour grade. Break the image into decisions.

#### Light

- Where is the main source relative to the face?
- Is it hard or soft?
- What happens in the eyes, under the nose and along the jaw?
- Is the background lighter or darker than the person?

#### Camera position

- Is the camera above, level with or below the subject?
- How close is it likely to be?
- Does the perspective feel wide and present or distant and compressed?
- Is the frame level, tilted, stable or moving?

#### Person and background

- How large is the person in the frame?
- How far are they from the background?
- Is the location recognisable, simplified or almost removed?
- What foreground or leading lines create depth?

#### Exposure and colour

- Is the face bright, neutral or deliberately dark?
- Which two or three colours dominate?
- Is the skin warmer or cooler than the environment?
- Does colour contrast separate the person or unify the scene?

#### Emotion and timing

- What is the person doing rather than merely how are they posed?
- Is the expression open, private, tense, playful or calm?
- Does the frame show the peak of an action or a moment just before or after it?

Write the answers in plain language. “Nice film look” is not an analysis. “Soft
side window light, eye-level camera, dark green background, warm face and a
quiet expression between movements” is something a photographer can attempt.

### 3. Recreate

Recreation does not mean copying the person's identity, body or exact pose. It
means rebuilding the relationships identified in the analysis with the
location, equipment and partner available to you.

Prioritise three decisions:

1. the direction and quality of light;
2. camera position and relationship to the background; and
3. the action or emotional temperature of the moment.

Colour and editing can refine the result later. They should not be used to hide
a completely different light or composition.

:::tip[Copy the decision, not the surface]
If the reference uses a professional model, adapt the direction to your partner
rather than demanding the same expression or body control. The recreation is
successful when the style survives while the person still feels like herself.
:::

## Analyse What You Already Like

Taste often appears before vocabulary. A student may repeatedly save soft
backlit portraits, wide environmental frames or direct-flash night photographs
without noticing the pattern.

Review ten saved portraits and group them by visible decisions:

- light direction;
- distance and focal-length feeling;
- amount of background;
- brightness and contrast;
- dominant colours;
- movement or stillness; and
- relationship between subject and camera.

The repeated pattern is more informative than the photographer's label. If
eight images use quiet side light and restrained colour, “I like cinematic
photos” may be less precise than “I like faces emerging from a darker, simple
environment”.

## Style Must Still Suit the Person

A coherent visual idea can still be the wrong portrait for the subject. Return
to the Week 1 question: does she like how she is seen?

Before recreating a reference, ask:

- Which part of this image do you like: colour, angle, clothing, expression or
  atmosphere?
- Is there anything you would not want copied?
- Does this style suit the intended use of the photograph?
- How much experimentation feels comfortable today?

The photographer brings visual judgement. The subject brings knowledge of how
she wants to appear. Style should be negotiated between those views rather than
used to overrule one of them.

## Build a Style Recipe, Not a Preset

After a successful recreation, write a short recipe that could guide another
shoot:

1. **Feeling:** three words.
2. **Light:** direction, hardness and time or source.
3. **Camera:** height, distance and approximate view.
4. **Space:** subject-to-background relationship and composition.
5. **Exposure:** brighter, neutral or darker, with the face protected.
6. **Colour:** two or three dominant colours.
7. **Moment:** one action, prompt or emotional direction.

This is more useful than saving a final colour preset because it begins before
the shutter is pressed.

## Make One Intentional Variation

Recreation teaches observation. Variation begins authorship.

After making the closest useful version of the reference, change one important
decision while preserving the same overall feeling:

- move from centre composition to negative space;
- keep the light but include more environment;
- replace stillness with movement;
- change the dominant colour while keeping the contrast relationship; or
- move the camera closer or lower for a more immediate feeling.

Compare the reference, recreation and variation. Identify which parts were
essential to the style and which could change without losing it.

## The Beginning of a Personal Style

Personal style is not finding one trick and applying it to every person. It is
developing a set of preferences you understand well enough to repeat, adapt
and sometimes reject.

> Find what you like. Analyse the decisions that produced it. Recreate those
> decisions with care. Then change one of them for a reason.

Week 8 does not ask students to finish a permanent signature. It asks them to
stop hoping that “feeling” will appear during editing and start designing it
from light, space, exposure and the moment itself.
